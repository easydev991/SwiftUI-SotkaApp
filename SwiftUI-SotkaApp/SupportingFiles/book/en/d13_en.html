Day 13. How to learn pull-ups?





Today we've prepared a special infopost for those who still can't do full pull-ups on the bar, but really want to learn!

Let's start with the most important thing. The only reason pull-ups might not work is that the muscles simply don't have enough strength to do the exercise. It really is hard for a beginner, so you need to prepare the muscles and ligaments and make them stronger. Lead-up exercises will help with that.

What are lead-up exercises?


These are easier variations of exercises that reduce the load on one or several parameters. And if the target exercise is still too hard (physically, technically, or mentally), it makes sense to start training a simplified version of it. Let's explain using pull-ups as an example:

If you look at a person doing pull-ups from the side, you'll see that first they pull their body up, then they lower it down. And if you remember the school physics course, in this case you can use the formula for Work (A) done by a body:

A = F * S where:

A — work;

F — force (in our case that's mass (m) multiplied by gravitational acceleration (g));

S — distance (range of motion).

So work depends on two parameters — body weight and the distance that body is moved. Therefore as lead-up exercises you need to use pull-up variations that reduce one of these two parameters, or both at once.

Option A: reduce the mass


Of course it's more correct to talk not about reducing body mass, which you can only achieve by dropping extra weight, but about compensating part of the gravity acting on it. The principle is simple: reduce the effect of gravity — reduce the load.

You can achieve that either with a lever (Australian pull-ups, pull-ups with a chair) or with elastic force (pull-ups with resistance bands).

Australian pull-ups




To do this exercise you need to find a low bar you can hang from with your feet on the ground (the torso at a slight incline). Depending on the height of the bar the load will change: the lower the bar, the harder it will be.

In terms of biomechanics, Australian pull-ups also differ from regular ones in that the pull happens in the horizontal plane, not the vertical. But this variation of pull-ups is great both for people just starting their path in outdoor training and for experienced athletes polishing correct pull-up technique.

Pull-ups with a chair for support


Here it's simple: you take the bar with your hands and stand on a chair with your feet. But for this to become a good lead-up exercise, from session to session you need to do the following:

1. Try to shift as much body weight as possible from the legs onto the arms and back. It's important not to slack off and to try, first of all, to pull with the back and arms.

2. Build up the number of reps. The more pull-ups, the longer the muscles are under load and the sooner they'll get strong enough to let you do a classic pull-up.

Pull-ups with resistance bands




This exercise is essentially similar to the previous one, but the load on the arm and back muscles depends on the resistance of the band. As soon as you can consistently do 10 pull-ups with the band in each circuit, you should switch to a less stiff one.

Option B: reduce the distance


The second way to lower the load is to reduce the range of motion. To do that, we'll split the exercise into parts and work on them separately.

Negative pull-ups


Any exercise has two phases: the concentric (when the muscles tense) and the eccentric (when the muscles relax).

If you still can't do both pull-up phases at once (lift yourself up, then lower yourself down), you can start with "negative" pull-ups. You need to find a bar high enough that after you jump onto it, the chin is above the bar, and then lower yourself down as slowly as possible, tensing only the arm and back muscles.

Partial reps


You can go even further and split pull-ups not into two parts, but into four:

1. Concentric from straight arms to a 90-degree angle at the elbows;

2. Concentric from a 90-degree angle at the elbows to the chin above the bar;

3. Eccentric from the chin above the bar to a 90-degree angle at the elbows;

4. Eccentric from a 90-degree angle at the elbows to straight arms.

Ideally, of course, you need to do the exercise through a full range of motion, but that requires a certain strength and level of preparation, so don't get upset or worry if for now you can only do a pull-up at 1/2 or 3/4 of the full range. The main thing is to train, and with time the strength will come!

Secrets of the bar masters


If there are two parameters in the formula, you can only change those, right? Wrong! There's always a third option!

Underhand-grip pull-ups




In essence these are full classic pull-ups, but they're still easier to do than pull-ups with the standard overhand grip. An underhand grip, like a neutral one where the palms are parallel to each other, lets you involve the arm muscles more. By the way, most people do their first pull-up with an underhand grip!

We recommend combining overhand-grip lead-up exercises with attempts to do full pull-ups with an underhand grip (or a neutral grip) for a better result.

The correct pull


If you want to learn correct pull-up technique right away and make the learning process easier, the following tip will help. Every time you do pull-ups, you need to pull the bar toward you, not yourself toward the bar!

There's an analogy with moving heavy furniture, for example a sofa from one corner of the room to another. How does that happen? We grab the sofa with our hands and pull it toward us to shift it. The back muscles and biceps work actively. To pull up easily and confidently, you just need to repeat that natural movement pattern, only now with the bar.

Training on bars


There are many machines whose creators promise they either fully replace pull-ups or help you learn pull-ups from scratch. But training on them is a bad idea.

They isolate the target muscles from the rest of the body — that makes it easier, but worse. Meanwhile, in real pull-ups, besides the large muscles of the back, shoulders, and biceps, a lot of stabilizer muscles are involved, helping keep the body straight and coordinated. That is, the whole body works. So we advise doing pull-ups on a regular bar.

Grip training




And the last point for today. For many beginners, hanging on the bar on their own hands is a separate problem. If that's an issue, we'd recommend adding a simple hang on the bar for 30–120 seconds (depending on your level) at the end of each workout (after all the circuits!) as a strengthening exercise for the grip. You can split the exercise into sets if you can't do it in one go.

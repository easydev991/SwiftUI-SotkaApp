Day 11.

Day 11. Winter and summer training





Street Workout is an outdoor subculture, which means weather affects training. It can be cold or hot outside, but that doesn't mean you should cancel the session. It only means you need to use your head and make the right adjustments. In today's infopost we'll look at a few simple recommendations that will let you train outdoors comfortably year-round!

When it's COLD


Even when the temperature outside drops below minus 15–20 degrees, you can still keep training outdoors. You'll be surprised, but sessions in frost sometimes feel much nicer than in summer heat. After all, putting on one more layer and warming up is much easier than cooling down under a blazing sun.

Benefits of winter training


1. Hardening the body. Winter outdoor training will help harden the body and avoid those annoying colds.

2. Continuity of the training process. By not taking a forced pause in training in winter, you keep progressing and in spring you don't face the need to start from scratch. In other words, you start preparing for the new beach season in the fall, not — like everyone else — a month before.

3. Unforgettable impressions. Imagine: the sun is just rising over the rooftops, steam is coming from your mouth, freshly fallen snow is crunching under your feet, and instead of staying in a warm bed you're already at the playground, conquering new heights.

4. Training willpower, grit, and determination. Comments unnecessary.

5. Fresh frosty air. It wakes you up and lifts your mood.

Logically, the main problem of training in the cold season is temperature. And it carries several dangers at once that you should pay attention to. Almost all of them are fixed, to one degree or another, by what you wear.

Rules of WINTER training!




#1. Don't overheat!

A common mistake of beginner winter athletes is fear of the frost and too much clothing. As a result the person starts sweating heavily and feels uncomfortable.

Dress for a winter session so that you feel slightly cold if you're standing still and doing nothing. But don't worry, because once you start doing exercises you'll warm up quickly!

#2. Layers

In winter you should wear several layers, each with a specific job. The first (bottom) layer is base-layer thermal wear that wicks moisture off the body. A full onesie is best (not a top and pants separately), because during the session we move a lot and the top can ride up, exposing the lower back. The second layer is fleece, which holds heat. The third (outer) layer protects from wind, rain, or snow. You pick it based on the weather that day.

#3. Full protection

Pay separate attention to exposed body parts: hands, ears, nose. If it's already below zero outside, especially with a strong wind, you must take care of the head and extremities. Even during the most intense sessions the ears and nose are still at risk of frostbite, so don't forget a hat, and in hard frost a special mask, balaclava or buff, or a warm scarf. And of course the fingers. They already take a beating during training as it is, and holding a metal bar at −20 degrees is not the most pleasant thing in the world, so it's worth getting special gloves for winter training.

#4. Warm doesn't mean expensive!

The internet is full of sites advertising special branded clothing for training in the cold. As a rule it costs a lot of money and comes with simply fantastic descriptions of how effective it is. Buying that high-tech gear is useful, but by no means a critical condition for successful winter training. The main thing to remember is to prefer synthetic fabrics that don't soak up moisture and dry quickly.

#5. Warm up the "engine"!

In winter the importance of a warm-up multiplies, because the body is about to work in the cold!

For anyone who drives, here's an analogy. Starting a car engine at −30 and at +30 is, as they say in Odessa, two big differences. In the cold, after starting the engine you begin moving smoothly, warming all the parts and mechanisms. For the human body in winter training that's no less relevant. It's better to do the warm-up at home already, so that when you walk out into the cold you start training right away. If the playground with bars is far from home, you can start the session with an easy run.

#6. Winter is outside

When you train in the cold, the body spends a lot of energy maintaining working temperature. During rest between circuits, never stand still, so you don't freeze. Walking and arm swings won't let the body cool down.

We also want to draw separate attention to the problem of ice. It's a really serious danger that can wait under fresh snow in winter. So jump off the bar or dip bars carefully, watch where you step during lunges, and also during a warm-up run.

You can inhale frosty air only through the nose — that will help avoid colds and infections tied to breathing cold air for a long time.

#7. Ending the workout

After the session, don't stay outside in a heated state with no physical activity. When you finish training, try to get into a warm room right away. If home is far, a run will make a great cool-down, and once you're back you can take a warm shower and drink hot tea. That will be an excellent end to the workout.

Conclusion


Winter training isn't an extreme sport, but it still has its differences from regular sessions. Listen to how you feel, don't set yourself sky-high tasks, and don't be ashamed to cancel training in really bad weather or if you feel unwell. Be sensible. Sometimes it's better to do a light session at home or skip the workout altogether than to go down with pneumonia for a whole month.

P.S. And a couple of last tips. First, if a couple of minutes before you go out you throw your kit on the radiator, going outside will be much nicer. Second, if you run in winter, finish the run with the wind so you don't choke on a cold stream of air.

Training in the HEAT


Specifics and conditions of summer training


Spring and summer are the most active and, in every sense, hottest time in Street Workout. While many sports go into an off-season lull, a real boom starts at outdoor playgrounds: wintered-over workouters show up, random "snowdrops" who decided to build a "beach body" in a month, and also winter-sport athletes who can only train GPP in summer. Training in summer is easy, fun, and pleasant, but there are certain points we want to draw your attention to today.

Let's immediately define what you should be wary of during summer training: high air temperature and humidity, direct sun, dehydration, getting chilled, and somewhat toxic fumes. Now, in order.

High air temperature and humidity




Our body is a self-regulating system that maintains a constant internal environment (homeostasis), and one of the most important indicators of that is body temperature, which affects absolutely all chemical reactions in the body. So a prolonged deviation (up to 24 hours) of body temperature from the norm of just 4–5 degrees leads to a fatal outcome.

To keep body temperature constant, the body has a number of mechanisms: vessel tone to redistribute blood between cold and warm areas, sweating, shivering, and so on. Remember that body temperature depends on two processes happening in parallel: heat production and transferring it to the environment. At rest, 2/3 of the heat of the human body is produced by the internal organs, 1/3 by the muscles. During training the situation changes radically, and the muscles already produce the lion's share of the heat — that is, heat production of the whole body increases 2–3 times. Heat is given off in 3 ways: conduction, convection, and radiation. Without going into a school physics course, we'll note that the critical air temperature is 28–32 degrees. When that mark is reached, and even more so when it's exceeded, the body sharply loses the ability to give off heat. In fact, when air temperature is higher than skin temperature, we have only one heat-loss mechanism left — conduction (via sweating and lung ventilation). And the effectiveness of that mechanism depends very strongly on air humidity: for example at 100% humidity, sweat evaporation won't happen at all, meaning the danger of overheating and heat stroke rises sharply when air temperature reaches skin temperature and/or humidity is 80–100%.

Direct sunlight




Direct sunlight can heat objects almost without limit. Train in the morning or evening, when the sun is just rising or already starting to set. That way you lower the risk of heat stroke and sunstroke. Their symptoms: headache, dizziness, sluggishness, a feeling of fatigue or anxious agitation, disorientation and confusion. In severe cases — convulsions, loss of consciousness, hallucinations. A rapid pulse is possible, high body temperature, the skin becomes dry and hot.

If someone around you gets heat stroke or sunstroke, call emergency services immediately. Before the doctors arrive, move the person into a cool room or shade, give them water, and put a cold compress on their head.

Dehydration




In summer the body increases sweating to keep body temperature at a normal level. Accordingly, if that fluid isn't replaced in time, the risk of dehydration rises. Even mild dehydration increases joint wear and the chance of injury, and reduces the muscles' ability to recover after training.

Given the dehydration danger mentioned above, the drinking recommendations are as follows: drink a moderate amount of water an hour before training (about 0.5 L is enough). Drink water during the workout every 10–15 minutes in small portions. Drink mineral water or cold tea after the workout.

Getting chilled




In summer after a workout you sometimes really want a cold shower or a dip in a lake. That's not an obvious danger, but it's still a common one.

Training raises body temperature and stimulates metabolism. The heart increases its rate and blood circulates through the body at high speed. To cope with the risen pressure, the vessels widen. But as soon as the body is immersed in cold water, the vessels start narrowing rapidly and blood pressure jumps. That's a big stress for the body — pressure can suddenly spike, and you can also simply catch a cold, because at that moment the body's ability to resist illness drops sharply.

Harmful fumes




Smog, greenhouse gases, and other "achievements" of civilization have long been faithful companions of big-city residents. Because of high temperature and humidity in summer, the concentration of these substances in the air multiplies. So if your sports playground starts smelling of various unpleasant aromas in summer, you should find yourself another place to train.

Specifics of training programs


In winter, because of eating habits and also less movement, there's a natural accumulation of body mass. In spring and summer, on the contrary, fruit and vegetables appearing in the diet lower its calorie content, and increased activity leads to extra calorie burn. So it's logical to do natural things through the year: in winter — actively build muscle, in summer — drop fat. Your training programs should be adapted to these goals. That is, in summer you can train less with extra weight, and instead work on improving GPP and strength endurance. Nobody forbids doing everything the exact opposite way, of course — but why?

So how do you adapt your program for summer? Remember the extra load on the heart. Watch how you feel and your pulse. You can increase rest time between sets to lower the intensity, or split your usual workout into 15–30-minute chunks that you'll do several times through the day. You can also drop accessory exercises and focus on basic multi-joint ones that will work the maximum number of muscle groups at once. Work with such training-process numbers as duration and number of sessions, number and composition of exercises, number of reps and sets, rest time between sets. Remember that the only pain you should feel is muscle pain (not joint pain, not a headache, not a stabbing or aching pain in the heart).

Recommendations for SUMMER training


1. It's preferable to train in the morning (before 10:00) or in the evening (after 18:00), avoiding the need to train under direct sun and choosing playgrounds that have at least partial shade.

2. Drink enough fluid before training and always take water with you to the session.

3. Headwear and clothing should be light-colored and, preferably, made of "cool" fabrics (cotton, linen). Don't use antiperspirants before training, because they hinder sweating and increase the chances of heat stroke.

4. Intensity and duration of the workout should be reduced if you feel worse or your numbers drop in the usual modes.

The main thing is to remember that we train in order to be healthy, strong, and active, so enthusiasm for training should be moderate.

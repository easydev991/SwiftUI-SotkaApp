Day 48. Fat-burning workouts (the myth of long cardio)




!!! Today we rest or do a light session before tomorrow's max testing !!!


If you look on Instagram or YouTube, every other fitness blogger has a video or post showing "the most effective fat-burning workout in just 5–10–15 minutes!" And in absolutely every case, inside you find some variation of high-intensity interval training after which you'll just be pouring sweat. But the problem is that sweat is not fat, and workouts like that have nothing to do with fat burning.

The popularity of those videos and posts is obvious: every year there are more and more chubby people around who are in a permanent search for a way to get in shape, but without spending a lot of time, changing their lifestyle, or bothering at all. Of course they're ready to pay for that. But since we're writing this article as part of the SOTKA program, we'll have to deny them the wish to throw their money to the wind and introduce them to harsh reality.

We want to answer the main question of the infopost right away: "Do fat-burning workouts exist?" Yes, they do. For that you need to train without stopping for at least 60 minutes in a low heart-rate zone — meaning no shortness of breath. We'll talk in more detail about levels of physical load, heart-rate zones, and the anaerobic threshold very soon in the ADVANCED block. And if you want to teach your body to use fats more effectively as an energy source, you need to train regularly for at least 120 minutes without rest.

Why so long? It's tied to the features of the muscle energy systems (there'll be a separate infopost about those too), for which it's much simpler, more effective, and faster to use not fats (from subcutaneous stores) as an energy source, but glucose (stored in the muscles and liver as glycogen). And the stores the body has are enough to fuel work for about 60 minutes.

The myth about workout length:
By the way, this is exactly where we can trace the roots of the myth that a workout shouldn't last more than an hour. Although the people who spread it completely forgot that each muscle has its own glycogen store, and so a trained person may have enough of those stores for 2 or even 3 hours. That's exactly what lets professional athletes train twice a day for several hours, smiling as they listen to fitness trainers say you can't train more than an hour.


But that's still only half the trouble. The second half is that the speed of fat oxidation itself is very low. Meaning that during any more or less intense work the body simply won't have time to use fats as an energy source (that's why we talked about low-intensity load above). So you can forget the dream that during a short, intense session, like the ones fake fitness gurus regularly push on you, you'll be able to burn even a little fat — because the body won't even start unpacking its stores!

So how do you still make the body start spending unneeded fat reserves? It's simple! You need to give it time for fat oxidation. So the most effective fat-burning workout is a calm run, an unhurried bike ride, or steady swimming, when you don't get short of breath and the pulse doesn't rise above a certain level. The length of a session like that should be at least 60 minutes, and better — twice as long.

In 1978, at the Laboratory for the Study of Human Muscle Activity, a study was done with Tom Osler, a world-famous marathon and ultramarathon runner. Tom was given an interesting assignment: to run and walk continuously for 72 hours, during which the energy systems of his muscles were studied. The measurements showed that during the first several hours of work (!) the athlete's muscles used mainly carbs as an energy source. And then they started switching more and more to using fats. In the last 24 hours of the study, fats already provided almost the entire volume of energy needed for the load, even though Tom constantly drank milk with sugar and ate holiday pie. Even though he ate food whose total energy value exceeded 9,000 kcal, in the end he had to drop out of the distance 2 hours before the end of the experiment, in a state of complete exhaustion and depleted energy stores. By that moment he had run 200 miles (~320 kilometers). [1]

Let's be realists — a training mode like that is incredibly boring, monotonous, and takes a hell of a lot of time, so it will only suit a very small number of people. So small that we can say with a clear conscience that no fat-burning workouts exist, because for the overwhelming majority of people they really are beyond what's available.

It's much simpler, more pleasant, and more effective to use training time not to "burn fats," but to develop various useful qualities — strength, endurance, speed, agility, flexibility, and so on. And to regulate the amount of fat in the body through diet and a sensible approach to nutrition — luckily we covered that question in detail back in week three!

Day 72. Types of muscle fibers and their energy supply





Thanks to ads we know that not all yogurts are equally useful, and thanks to the SOTKA program you will learn today that not all muscle fibers in our body are the same.

Of course you can train based on your own experience and empirical knowledge (and sometimes that is exactly what you should do), but it is also very important to understand what stands behind the empirical results. For that you need to sort out the theoretical side, and today we start a series of posts in which we will lean on scientific foundations, on physiology and biochemistry as they apply to training. Let's say it up front: there are two main classifications of muscle fibers — by contraction speed and by type of energy supply. Let's look at each of them in more detail.

Classification of muscles by contraction speed


Globally, skeletal-muscle fibers come in two kinds: slow and fast. And in different people they sit in the body in different ratios — some have more of one, some of the other. For example, if there are more slow fibers, the person is more inclined to become a long-distance runner, and if there are more fast ones, a sprinter. That is set genetically, and unfortunately you cannot do anything about it.

Type I: Slow muscle fibers (red)


The contraction speed of this type of fiber is fairly low, but they can contract continuously for a long time thanks to oxygen oxidation (more on that below).

Red fibers have a small diameter, are surrounded by a mass of capillaries (tiny blood vessels), and contain a lot of the protein myoglobin, which stores oxygen and gives it to the mitochondria (in fact it serves to transport oxygen) if not enough is coming with the blood. The numerous mitochondria (energy stations — more on them later) of red fibers have a high level of oxidative-enzyme activity, which lets them turn carbohydrates and fatty acids into energy.

Type II: Fast muscle fibers (white)


The contraction speed of this type of fiber is fairly high (40–100% of maximum), but they are not designed for long work because of how they are supplied with energy. Energy supply happens through anaerobic glycolysis (more on that below too), and that mechanism works only for a very short time (about 30–60 seconds).

White muscle fibers have a large diameter. They contain a large amount of glycogen (a complex carbohydrate made of glucose, and representing the body's energy reserves), while there are few mitochondria. Among the enzymes, glycolytic ones dominate (they break glycogen down to glucose — the main energy source of our body).

Two subtypes of white muscle fibers are singled out separately: subtype IIa and IIb.

Subtype IIa (intermediate fibers)


These muscle fibers can use both oxygen and oxygen-free metabolism equally as an energy-supply mechanism. In essence such fibers are something in between fast and slow (from 25% to 40% of maximum contraction).

Subtype IIb


These are the true fast muscle fibers. They use only oxygen-free metabolism. They have no myoglobin protein at all, but they have maximum strength and contraction speed.

Classification of muscles by type of energy supply


The main fuel for every cell structure is adenosine triphosphate, ATP for short. ATP is known as the universal energy source for all biochemical processes that run in living systems. ATP is obtained from all the other sources that a person cannot use directly, and has to use to refill ATP stores. You can compare it to processing different kinds of fuel — coal, peat, diesel, and so on — into electricity, which you can then use as power for everything.

After the ATP stores in the muscles are used up, and that happens in just a couple of seconds, the body starts looking for sources to refill ATP and keep working.

The first source used to resynthesize ATP is creatine phosphate (CrP). That energy-supply mechanism is called the creatine-phosphokinase mechanism. And although its stores in the muscles are several times larger than the ATP stores, it also lasts a very short time (about 30–60 seconds). You can picture this source as a battery whose charge, though, is fairly small. But when creatine-phosphate energy is used, free creatine and free phosphate appear in the body, and they stimulate the activity of other energy-supply mechanisms.

Those mechanisms can be split into two groups:

- Ones that do not use oxygen (anaerobic)

- Ones that use oxygen (aerobic)

We will try to explain each of them as simply as we can. Let's start with the anaerobic one, whose scheme looks like this:



After creatine phosphate (CrP) has run out, the body starts using glucose stored in glycogen to create ATP molecules that will go toward refilling creatine-phosphate stores. And creatine phosphate, in turn, will already refill the ATP stores that were spent while the muscle fiber worked.

In parallel, pyruvate (pyruvic acid, a product of glucose metabolism) and hydrogen ions form. If the body does not manage to neutralize the growing number of hydrogen ions, they accumulate and start causing that burning feel in the muscle that many of you have met while doing exercises. It is worth noting that as they accumulate, hydrogen ions start destroying molecules inside the protein, which can lead to its death.

It is the accumulation of hydrogen ions to a maximum that is the limit that restricts how long this energy-supply mechanism can be used. In numbers, that takes only about 60 seconds.

Now let's move to the second mechanism. The scheme of aerobic glycolysis looks like this:



Unlike the previous option, this scheme also has a new element called mitochondria. In fact these are the energy stations of the cell, whose main job is to oxidize organic compounds and use the energy released when they break down to synthesize ATP.

Thanks to them, the pyruvate that forms during glycolysis, instead of turning into lactate and leading to an accumulation of hydrogen ions, will be sent inside the mitochondrion and turn into CO2, H2O, and ATP molecules, which will also go toward restoring creatine-phosphate (CrP) stores. And because hydrogen ions do not form, the environment does not acidify and the muscles can contract for a very long time.

If you simplify it a lot, our endurance depends on the number of mitochondria in the cells: the more mitochondria, the more endurance we will have, and the more reps, for example, we can do.

Types of muscle fibers: GMF and OMF


Muscle fibers that have few mitochondria, and that are therefore supplied with energy the oxygen-free way, are called glycolytic muscle fibers (GMF).

When hydrogen ions are accumulated to a maximum, the muscle fibers stop contracting altogether, and the person feels sharp local fatigue.

Muscle fibers that have many mitochondria, and that are therefore supplied with energy the oxygen way, are called oxidative muscle fibers (OMF). OMF do not acidify, which means they are almost unfatiguable. What is more, aerobic glycolysis is about 18–20 times more effective than anaerobic, because from one glucose molecule it gets 38 ATP molecules versus 2!

And now two pleasant points for you:

1. If the ratio of fast and slow muscle fibers depends on genetics, then by the glycolytic and oxidative principle muscle fibers are not inherited.

2. The number of mitochondria in a muscle fiber can be increased! And that is exactly what you need to do if you want to do more reps in the exercises. But you can do it, of course, only within certain frames laid into us genetically.

Conclusion


Now you know that your muscles are made of two types of fibers that were designed to do different types of work. Some are used when the body needs a lot of strength in a short time, others when you do not need that much strength, but for a long time. So to develop fully you need to include load in your training for both the first and the second (shifting the emphasis depending on individual goals).

But do not mix the two classifications together and conclude that fast fibers are always glycolytic, and slow ones oxidative.

And if genetics (the FMF and SMF ratio) cannot be remade, then in terms of the energy supply of your muscle fibers (and therefore your endurance), it is the opposite. And the number of OMF can be grown. But for that you need correctly built workouts, and we will still talk about them in one of the next infoposts!

Day 44. Sleep





How important is sleep for getting the results you want? Take a mobile phone as an example — it works all day, and with extra features too: WIFI, GPS, Internet, and so on. Even the most modern models will last no more than 12 hours with that kind of use. And then? Then you need to put the phone on charge for the night. Well, the situation with a person is exactly the same! And if you don't recharge yourself regularly, you won't be able to work at full power!



Sleep is a key part of rest. During sleep, many vital functions recover in the body, especially those tied to the nervous system (in particular, neural networks and the nerve centers that manage metabolism and the hormonal system get reorganized). And although many people think sleep is a passive process, because the muscles relax, the heart rate slows, and breathing becomes less frequent, at the same time the endocrine glands work harder and a number of anabolic (building) processes switch on. But all of that only happens if the person gets enough sleep. A lack of sleep leads to a rise in so-called stress hormones (for example, cortisol) and speeds up catabolic (breakdown) processes.

Besides that, a lack of sleep negatively affects reaction speed to outside stimuli, activity level, and a person's cognitive abilities. What's especially interesting is that this happens without the person noticing, which has been confirmed in experiments. So to give your maximum during the day, it's critically important to give your body enough sleep.

But what does "enough" mean? There's a widespread opinion that an adult needs at least 8 hours of sleep, and a child at least 10. You can forget that information, because it doesn't match reality. So the next time someone says they sleep 6–7 hours a day and feel rested, you don't need to think they're abnormal. They just managed to organize their daily schedule effectively and set up their sleep mode.

Sleep phases


When we talk about sleep, the first thing to say is that it can be split into several clearly marked phases:

Phase #1: Light drowsiness. You're probably familiar with the feeling of sitting in front of a computer while your eyes close on their own, or listening to a monotonous, lulling lecture at university and then coming to and realizing 5 minutes have already passed.

Phase #2: Light sleep. This is already real sleep, characterized by rare bursts of brain activity.

Phase #3: Deep sleep. This is the phase where the brain and body relax and the most intense recovery of the body's resources happens, so it's the most important one. It is also characterized by almost no brain activity, a drop in heart rate, and a drop in body temperature.

Phase #4: REM sleep. In this phase the body's rest continues, but it's not as intense as in deep sleep. By the way, dreams most often appear in this phase, which is why it also got the name "Rapid Eye Movement" — because during it the pupils make very fast movements under the eyelids.

It's worth noting that this four-phase cycle doesn't happen once, but several times during sleep. And with each following cycle, the length of the third phase shrinks and the length of the others grows. In the very first cycle, the third phase is the longest.

Melatonin:
Heard of this hormone? If we describe its effect on the body in two words, the more of it is released, the more we want to sleep. And it starts being released actively in conditions where our eyes don't get enough light. That's a mechanism nature built in so it's easier for us to fall asleep when night comes. But if there's a bright light source nearby, this hormone breaks down, which is why people in big cities almost universally suffer from sleep problems.


From that follows a simple conclusion — the more DEEP SLEEP you have, and the DEEPER it is, the more rested and recovered you will be. And you should remember the dual nature of physical states that we already talked about in one of the previous infoposts.

Tips for people who have trouble sleeping and falling asleep


1. Build the habit of going to bed at the same time. At first it may be hard and you'll have to force yourself, but over time the force of habit will put you to bed on its own.

2. Use the bed only for sleep. It's best to create a stable association in the body of the bed as a place for sleep and rest. So don't turn it into a home office, don't read in bed, and so on.

3. Get rid of extra thoughts. If you often catch yourself unable to fall asleep because the brain keeps thinking about something, pay more attention to what you do 30–60 minutes before sleep. Take out of that time all activities that can stir the brain up (computer work, watching TV, playing on a smartphone, solving a Rubik's cube).

4. Lower your body temperature. Since the very start of sleep is characterized by a drop in activity and body temperature, you can make the transition to sleep easier if you air the room well (it's easier to fall asleep in a cool bed, but the temperature should be comfortable, so don't freeze yourself), start behaving more calmly yourself, listen to some relaxing music, and so on.

5. Go to bed in complete darkness. As we said above, a light source — even something as simple as a street lamp outside the window — will get in the way of falling asleep, because it will block melatonin production. So blackout curtains are your friend.

Day 76. Ligaments and tendons




Muscles are the main driving force of the body. By contracting and relaxing, they move the bones of the skeleton through space. That is why, when people talk about training, they think about muscles first. But muscles do not exist on their own. They attach to bones through tendons. Bones connect to each other with ligaments, and a movable complex of several bones is called a joint. To keep the risk of injury as low as possible, you also need a basic picture of these structures.

Why joints do not really “exist”?


Joints are the connections of two or more bones


That is the definition from the Merck medical manual, and it already shows the point: unlike bones, muscles, ligaments and tendons, a joint is not a separate body part. It is a mental construct that makes it easier to talk about how the body is built. In practice, “joint” is just the name of the connection between two or more bones.

What are we talking about?


Ligaments are strong but elastic connections between bones. They stabilize the joint and stop it from moving in the “wrong” direction.

Ligament and tendon tissue contains collagen and elastic fibers, but there is more collagen than elastin.

Collagen:
A protein that forms the base of connective tissue (tendon, bone, ligament, cartilage, dermis, and so on) and gives it strength and elasticity.


Elastin:
An elastic protein that can stretch a lot (relative deformation of 200–300%).


Collagen and elastin content in ligament tissue can differ a lot from person to person, depending on body type and age. That is one reason some people are naturally more flexible than others.

Ligaments are elastic, so they can stretch when a joint moves. When someone says they “pulled a ligament in training,” they mean something else. When ligaments stretch inside their natural range, you do not even notice it. Pain appears when they are forced beyond that range. Then they tear, and recovery can take months. Treat ligament injuries at least as seriously as fractures!

Tendons are the part of a muscle that attaches it to bone. Their main job is to transfer force from muscle to bone. Almost every human movement depends on that.

Tendons are made of thick structural units — fibrils packed tightly into bundles — and those fibrils contain collagen fibers. Collagen’s key property is high tensile strength with little relative deformation.

The mechanical properties of ligaments and tendons depend on their size and composition. A larger cross-section and a higher share of collagen fibers mean more strength. Hormone levels and mobility also matter. Immobilization significantly reduces ligament and tendon strength.

An important point: in most cases a tendon is stronger than the place where it attaches to bone. So in injuries tendons usually do not tear in the middle — they rip off at the attachment. Regular training builds ridges and bumps at those attachment points and increases the contact area. But tendon and ligament strength grows much more slowly than muscle strength. If you force speed-strength qualities too fast, the muscles can outrun the tendons and ligaments. That is a recipe for injury.

Ligament training


You cannot train ligaments. The internet is full of myths, so many beginners think they can “make ligaments more flexible” with stretching. As we already said in the stretching infopost, you are not stretching ligaments — you are learning to relax muscles on purpose. Flexibility improves because muscles relax. Ligament elasticity is mostly set at the genetic level.

Tendon training (the myth)


Anyone who has looked into tendon training has seen the name Alexander Ivanovich Zass, also known as Iron Samson. In the early 20th century he promoted his original system of static chain exercises. The motto was: “Muscles alone will not hold horses pulling in opposite directions, but tendons will. You need to train them, you need to develop them, and there is a way to strengthen them.”

In reality, muscles produce the force, and muscles are what would hold those horses. Tendons only transfer force. Think of a tower crane as a simplified model (it ignores nerves and reflexes, but it still helps). The cable is the tendon, the motor is the muscles. Whether the load leaves the ground depends on the motor. The cable is under load either way — whether the crane is lifting or the load is just hanging.

Zass was wrong. Tendons are passive connective-tissue elements that transmit force. The actual strength work is done by muscles applying effort.

Conclusion


Many people start training by thinking only about muscles and never notice the other structures that also take load and also need recovery time. Ligament and tendon injuries are serious. From day one, learn to listen to your body and do not overload yourself when you feel deeply tired.

Day 41. Myths about strength and cardio





When it comes to training, there are so many myths, misconceptions, and inaccuracies that you have to wonder how people manage to progress at all. In today's infopost we've picked out the 3 most popular myths about strength training and the 6 most popular myths about cardio that we want to share with participants of our program!

Strength training




Myth #1: Big muscles need big weights, and light weights will only give you definition

Since this myth has two parts, we'll look at it in two parts as well. Let's start with the first, which says that if we want big muscles, we absolutely have to lift big weights.

To be honest, your body doesn't care what weight you lift, because it doesn't think in the spirit of: "Nah, this guy didn't put enough plates on himself/the bar, so this time I won't grow." Your body thinks in the spirit of: "What is this guy doing? I don't have enough muscle to handle loads like this, something has to change!" Muscle growth is a consequence of adaptation — the process of your body adjusting to the outside environment and its conditions. For muscles to start growing, you need to create a need in the body to build them!

That need is created during training. In general, physical exercise leads to all kinds of changes (there's a long list), but the main thing is this: if the load in the session was above the level the body is used to, it will start the general adaptation mechanism — not only to that specific load, but in general.

First the body will restore all systems to the previous level (that's called recovery), and then it will keep strengthening them further (that's called supercompensation). It's a bit like defending a fortress: you've just beaten off another enemy attack and decided you should strengthen the existing defenses — for the future. Anyone who's played Tower Defence will get what we mean. And weight is only one possible parameter for changing the volume and intensity of the load, and probably the most obvious one, but far from the only one. There will be more on this in the ADVANCED block, and we move on.

That's about big muscles. But our myth also has a second part, dedicated to so-called "definition." And here we also have a lot of new things to open up.

First, muscles can't be defined or undefined, shapeless or "worked." Muscles can only be big or small, and strong or weak. Under the layer of skin and fat, absolutely all muscle fibers are always defined, dry, and worked! So when people talk about definition, they should talk about how defined the body looks (with clearly visible muscles) or the lack of that definition. That, in turn, depends both on the amount of subcutaneous fat sitting over the muscles (more fat — less definition) and on the size of the muscle (a bigger muscle is more visible under the fat).

REMEMBER!!!
The only things we can do with muscles are make them bigger (hypertrophy) or smaller (atrophy), stronger or weaker.

A muscle can't change its shape (which is set genetically), unless it is made of fibers or bundles that run in different directions and perform different movements of the bones they're attached to with tendons.

The only thing we can do with our muscle is try to make the whole thing bigger. All that variety of muscle shapes you see in people on outdoor grounds or in videos online is not because they train them differently — it's their genetics. One person has a short, peaked biceps, another has a long, sloping one that ends almost in the elbow crease. One person's lats drop like a cobra hood almost to the pelvis, another's are so short they look like lumps under the armpits pushing the arms out to the sides.


So the only thing (on the training side) that matters for people who want to build muscle is picking the right exercises. And "right" means they do their main job — create a need for the body to adapt. That will depend on different factors, and we'll come back to this in the ADVANCED block.

Globally, muscle growth (hypertrophy) can be triggered by any training mode: high-volume (lots of reps) or high-intensity. The difference is that different parts of the muscle fiber will grow: the myofibrils (the contractile elements), the mitochondria (its little power stations), the sarcoplasm (the intracellular fluid with everything in it), or other muscle elements. We'll talk about all of this in the ADVANCED block too, so don't worry if these words seem new and unclear right now.

It's also worth noting that there are (almost) no exercises that are absolutely useful or useless, effective or ineffective. Here you should also start from the level of the person doing the exercises. For a beginner who decided to start with SOTKA and has a low level of coordination, joint mobility, and body awareness, the basic exercises that make up the core of the program — push-ups, pull-ups, and squats — will be more than enough.

Myth #2: Different exercises can load different parts of a muscle

Online you very often see the idea that to fully work a muscle you need to train it with different exercises, varying the "angles of attack," grip width, hand placement, and so on.

We'll say it straight: you can and should work a muscle from different angles only when it is made of bundles that run in different directions and perform different movements. For example, the pectoralis major has three bundles that perform different movements (upper — clavicular, middle — sternal, and lower — abdominal), so it makes sense to do push-ups in different planes (hands and feet at the same level, feet above hands, hands above feet). But at the same time, the latissimus dorsi is one unit (in the sense that all its bundles perform the same movement), so changing grip width on the bar to work the muscle across its whole width makes no sense. Because if all the bundles and fibers of a muscle perform one movement, you can't load a separate part of it!

Moving on, let's think about what happens to a muscle during an exercise. Picture a rubber band, stretched and attached to two planes joined by a hinge. As it shortens, the rubber will pull the planes toward each other (in anatomy that movement is called flexion) — and a contracting muscle does the same thing with the bones.

Attention, a question!
Can you imagine one part of the rubber being more tense than another? No? That's how a muscle will contract too — evenly, transferring force from one bone to the other.


This myth comes from the fact that during some exercises you get various painful sensations — burning, a "fire," a dull ache, pain from stretching forces — closer to the start of the muscle or to where it attaches. These kinds of pain can appear for different reasons: the muscle getting acidic (burning), maximum tension in a fully shortened state (dull ache, "soreness"), a significant stretch of a muscle that's under load. And where you feel the pain will depend more on where the receptors that register those states sit. For example, the receptors that register stretch of muscle fibers sit at the ends of the muscle, where it turns into tendon, and in the tendons themselves. So when you stretch the hamstrings, you'll feel pain in the tendons behind the knee, even though those muscles are stretching as a whole — from origin to insertion. The same is true of muscle pain for other reasons. So even though the whole muscle is tensing, we can feel pain in a separate part of it.

On the other hand, muscles get used to the same kind of load, so from time to time you need to change its character — the number of reps, the weight, the speed of movement, whether you let the muscle relax between reps or not, whether you use momentum, a peak isometric contraction, and other parameters. As for swapping one exercise for another, or six of one for half a dozen of the other — you can do that just to add some psychological variety to training.

Myth #3: You can reduce fat locally by doing exercises for certain body parts

YOU CANNOT REMOVE FAT LOCALLY from a specific area of the body! So if you want to take fat off so-called problem zones — the places with the most subcutaneous and visceral fat (usually the thighs, glutes, love handles, and belly) — you'll have to reduce the amount of fat in the body as a whole! The body didn't pick those places to store reserves for no reason: that's where the protective and insulating functions of body fat matter most. And it's simply convenient to carry it there because it's close to the center of gravity.

The process of storing fat, just like the process of breaking it down, is regulated in our body by hormones released into the blood and carried throughout the body. And the strength of the reaction to a hormone depends directly on the number of receptors that can make contact with a molecule of that specific hormone. In so-called problem zones, fat tissue has the most receptors responsible for lipogenesis (building fat cells) and the fewest for lipolysis (breaking them down). And that ratio will stay the same no matter which specific muscle groups have a need for extra energy.

Cardio training




Not many people like it, but everyone has heard how necessary it is for getting in shape. After all, if you run a lot or pedal a stationary bike for a long time, you'll definitely drop extra kilos and get slimmer. Isn't that what they write on the internet or in glossy magazines — in other words, in the main sources of fitness information for most people? Today you'll learn that it's actually not like that at all, and the information above is one of the typical misconceptions about cardio!

In general there are quite a few myths about aerobic work, so we'll look only at the ones you meet most often:

Myth #1: Aerobic work is all you need to lose extra weight

To lose extra weight you need to create a calorie deficit (spend more calories than you take in). If you have no diet or eating plan, and instead you chaotically eat whatever is in the fridge, then no matter how much you train, the extra weight isn't going anywhere.

Myth #2: Low-intensity aerobic work helps you lose more extra fat than high-intensity training

Low-intensity training uses fewer muscles than high-intensity training, and it also uses slow muscle fibers much more than fast ones. Because of that, calorie burn will be lower too.

And even though it's believed that during low-intensity training the body starts using fat as an energy source, you should also remember that it learns to store fat to the same degree, so it can use it as energy later. And that's a closed loop.

Myth #3: Cardio is a waste of time

If it sounded like aerobic work is useless — that's not the case at all. It's very important for everyone, because it trains your cardiovascular system, your respiratory system, and your endurance! Not the least useful qualities, right?

Myth #4: The more cardio, the better

Cardio becomes less and less effective over time because your body adapts to it and starts working (and using its resources) more efficiently.

If the goal is training endurance, the cardiovascular system, and the lungs — then yes. If you want to lose weight and get in shape, you should look for more effective methods.

Myth #5: The best kind of aerobic work is running

Running is the most common kind of cardio, and that makes a lot of people think it's the best. But that's not quite true. The best option is the one you'll like and that's safe for your health. Not everyone can run because of their health, but from the whole variety of possible aerobic activities you can pick ones you can do on a regular basis.

Myth #6: Cardio is dangerous for the joints

There are many aerobic activities that by their nature don't involve impact on the joints (unlike running). For example cycling, rollerblading, rowing, swimming, walking stairs, and so on. Not all aerobic work implies a chance of an orthopedic injury. Cycling, rowing, or a stair stepper are by definition non-impact (they don't hit the joints hard).

Besides that, many people tie the injury risk of cardio to the idea that you have to do it in crazy amounts — but we've already debunked that myth.

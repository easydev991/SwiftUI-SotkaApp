Day 16. What is food made of? Proteins, fats, carbs, and vitamins





Any food consists of two main components: organic substances (proteins, fats, carbohydrates, vitamins) and inorganic substances (minerals and other chemical substances, microelements, water)

And each component of food performs some important function in the body.

Functions of food:

1. Plastic (structural) — supplying the body with building material for creating and renewing cells and tissues. Proteins are used for this first of all, but also fats, and to a lesser extent carbs.

2. Bioregulatory — participation in forming enzymes and hormones that regulate our body's activity. Here proteins again play the deciding role; vitamins and fats are also involved.

3. Energetic — supplying the body with energy. Carbs and fats are spent on this function first of all, and in emergency situations — proteins.

Now let's look separately at the three pillars our food is made of (the so-called macronutrients) — Proteins, Fats, and Carbohydrates.

Interesting!
The first person to split food into its component parts was the American professor Wilbur Olin Atwater at the end of the 19th century. Later he was the one who made the first attempts to measure the caloric value of food by burning it in a bomb calorimeter.


Proteins




Proteins (from Greek protos — first) are the main component our body is made of. About 85% of all tissues of any living organism, including the human body, consist of protein.

All kinds of proteins consist of only 20 different amino acids, and 8 of them are essential. That means the body cannot synthesize them on its own and must get them from food.

5 important functions of proteins


1. Act as building material for living tissues.

2. Carry vitamins, hormones, fatty acids, and other substances throughout the body.

3. Ensure normal functioning of the immune system.

4. Support the work of the "heredity apparatus".

5. Help many chemical reactions that the body needs to stay alive.

To build new cells in place of destroyed ones, a person constantly needs proteins. Proteins are also the only source of nitrogen, without which the body cannot synthesize other proteins. Proteins can also be used by the body to get energy if it's lacking (oxidizing 1 g of protein produces 4.1 kcal), but using them for that purpose is like lighting a fire with 100-dollar bills.

The biological value of proteins in a given food is determined by two main characteristics: the number of essential amino acids and how easily they're absorbed. Proteins of animal origin have higher biological value (eggs, meat, fish, dairy), plant ones have lower (nuts, legumes, flour, vegetables and fruit). But to get all the essential amino acids and fully supply the body you need to consume a variety of protein foods. We'll talk about that more later this week.

A lack of protein in food leads to their breakdown in the tissues, when your own proteins and your own nitrogen are used for urgent needs. Here's an analogy. Imagine you're building a building, and at some point you have a brick shortage. But the work still has to be finished, so you decide to pull bricks out of already-built walls to put up new ones. When that happens to our body, nervous-system activity is disrupted, a hormonal imbalance appears, body weight drops, muscle breakdown starts and fat is deposited in the internal organs — instead of the destroyed bricks the body puts on wooden patches.

The other extreme is excessive protein intake. It overloads the liver and kidneys, which take part in neutralizing and disposing of protein-breakdown products (in the process of synthesizing other proteins). The risk of allergic reactions rises, and rotting processes in the intestine increase (digestive disorder).

Conclusion: it's much better to spread protein foods across the whole diet through the day than to eat one large portion at once.

Fats




Fats are part of all living tissues of our body, largely determining their structure and normal vital activity. The health of skin, hair, a person's appearance and how they feel depend on fats. Our ability to resist infections also depends on a sufficient amount of fat in the body.

6 important functions of fats


1. They are part of absolutely all cell membranes.

2. They help absorb fat-soluble vitamins and are part of bile, without which digestion is impossible.

3. They are an excellent energy source for the body. Per 1 gram of substance broken down they give twice as much energy as proteins or carbs.

4. They take part in the body's thermoregulation and a number of protective reactions.

5. They improve the taste of food. It's largely thanks to fats that we taste food (proteins have a neutral or even unpleasant taste, and almost all carbs have one taste — sweet).

6. And hormones are made of them too (including from cholesterol), among others the hormones responsible for fat loss.

Obviously fats should be in our daily diet, because without them the body cannot function normally. But it's not that simple. The fats we consume with food consist of fatty acids and glycerol. Fatty acids come in two types (a chemical characteristic — by the number of covalent bonds between carbon atoms in the molecule): saturated and unsaturated.

Unsaturated fats. Today they are considered the most useful for your body. They can be monounsaturated (found in olive oil, canola oil, olives, avocado, hazelnuts, pistachios, almonds, macadamia nuts, and so on) and polyunsaturated (found in various vegetable oils (sunflower, corn, cottonseed), fish (especially sturgeon and their caviar), seeds, and so on).

These fats are considered useful because they raise the level of "good" cholesterol (high-density lipoproteins) and lower the level of "bad" cholesterol (low-density lipoproteins).

High-density lipoproteins (HDL):
Well soluble and not prone to depositing cholesterol, and thereby protect the vessels from atherosclerotic changes (that is, they are not atherogenic)


Low-density lipoproteins (LDL):
Poorly soluble and prone to depositing cholesterol crystals, forming atherosclerotic plaques in the vessels — the cause of heart attacks and ischemic strokes.


Polyunsaturated fatty acids are not synthesized in the body — that is, we can only get them from food. And there are no products that contain all the kinds of fatty acids our body needs at once. So it's important to eat a variety of fat-containing foods, while giving preference to food rich in polyunsaturated fatty acids.

Saturated fats. At present they are considered conditionally bad, and that's because they raise the overall cholesterol level, including the "bad" kind. In large amounts they are found in products of animal origin (ham, pork lard, beef, butter, cheese, oil, cocoa, coconut, coconut milk, coconut oil, cream, cream cheese, ice cream, fatback, palm kernel oil, palm oil, game, sour cream, whole milk).

It's important to note that these are not harmful fats and you cannot fully exclude them from the diet (for example, our brain consists exactly of saturated fats), but you need to be quite moderate in consuming them.

Trans fats. This is the most harmful type of fat. A clear example is spreads (liquid unsaturated vegetable fats are turned into solid saturated ones). They not only raise the overall cholesterol level, they also lower the level of "good" cholesterol!

If you're interested in why they were created, the answer as always is simple — for money. Using industrially produced trans fats lets products sit longer on store shelves! By the way, it's exactly these trans fats you can find in all the food that's considered junk (fast food, chips, donuts, and so on), so be careful! At the same time trans fats can also be found in dairy products and the meat of ruminants; those are classed as naturally occurring trans fats.

Do you know the recommended intake of industrially produced trans fats? Zero grams. Zero! Because consuming trans fats even in small amounts raises the probability of heart disease and other problems tied to high blood cholesterol. For naturally occurring trans fats that figure is a bit higher — no more than 1% of the total daily diet.

And remember: regardless of whether fats are classed as "good" or "bad", they are still high-calorie substances, and excessive consumption leads to excess weight. According to the World Health Organization, "excessive consumption" means more than 30% of the total diet.

Carbohydrates




Carbs are the main energy source in the body, and at the same time one of the main sources of problems with extra weight.

3 important functions of carbohydrates


1. Act as an energy source for the body.

2. Take part in forming cells and tissues.

3. Are part of protoplasm, subcellular and cellular structures, and take part in the vital activity of cells.

Carbs supply energy to all cells and living tissues of the body, but the central nervous system needs them most. Our brain consumes about 100 g of pure glucose a day (380 kcal).

That's about the same as the minimum amount of carbs a day required to supply energy to tissues that process glucose. And the same amount is needed to lower the chance that, compensating for a lack of carbs, the body will start supplying itself with energy by breaking down proteins — this is also called the protein-sparing action of carbs.

Types of carbohydrates


Carbs can be simple or complex, depending on whether they consist of single molecules or chains of them.

Simple carbs are used mainly for direct energy supply, but they can also be stored as complex polysaccharides — in plants that's starch, and in animals and people — glycogen, which is stored in the liver and muscles.

It's glycogen stored directly in the muscles that is used in strength training for their energy supply. It's very important to control your diet for a sufficient amount of carbs. Otherwise, with a constant carb deficit in the diet, glycogen stores will gradually drop, which can lead to a buildup of chronic fatigue.

Complex carbs digest slowly, enter the blood gradually, and smoothly maintain the glucose level in it. They supply the body with energy for a long time. For example, carbs in pasta can be absorbed for up to 12 hours. So if you mainly choose complex carbs, the feeling of fullness will last much longer, appetite spikes will become fewer, and the desire to wolf down something sweet will go away.

Simple carbs cause a sharp spike in blood glucose. Complex carbs don't raise blood sugar as strongly, but over a longer time.

Vitamins


Vitamins are substances contained in food that are needed to form enzymes and other biologically active substances. Although they're used in very small amounts, their excess or lack can lead to serious disruptions in how the body works.

It should be noted that vitamins are destroyed during storage and culinary processing of food.

Functions of vitamins


1. Vitamin A. Affects growth and development, raises the body's protective properties.

2. B vitamins. Affect the work of the muscular and nervous systems.

3. Vitamin C. Strengthens the work of the body's immune system.

The list can go on for quite a while, and each vitamin will have its own specific function.

Water-soluble and fat-soluble vitamins


The main difference between the first (B and C in particular) and the second (A and D among them) is that some are better absorbed if you consume them with water, and others — if with fat. For example, carrots with sour cream will enrich the body with vitamin A much better than without sour cream.

Conclusion


Wrapping up today's infopost, we want to give simple analogies so this topic doesn't seem too hard for you, comparing different food components to different building materials:

Proteins are building blocks, bricks. They're used to build new cells of our body.

Fats are wood. It can be used for construction (you can make wooden walls), or it can be used to get energy (if you burn it). It's also important that wood can be stockpiled and stored.

Carbs are energy, electricity. In all cases when the body needs energy it will use carbs first. It's important that you can't store a lot of energy, although some reserve can be kept in batteries.

Vitamins and minerals are precious stones. Diamonds, for example, are needed in some areas of industry to create complex and durable tools. The body doesn't need a lot of these substances, but without them it can't cope.

It should be noted that our body can turn some kinds of food substances into others. From carbs it can synthesize any other carbs and some fats, from fats — other fats and carbs, and so on. But its abilities in this are quite limited. It can get proteins only from other proteins, not from fats and carbs. Besides that, there are some amino acids (a component of proteins), some fatty acids (a component of fats), and also some vitamins that the body cannot synthesize at all. They are called essential, and you can only get them from outside with food. There are no essential carbs — our body can get them from any other kinds of nutrients.

Continuing our metaphor, for normal housekeeping the body needs stones and wood, and also a small amount of diamonds. They should be high quality and of decent origin. To process them and turn them into each other you need enough energy. It's important that there's enough of everything, so a certain amount of especially valuable, essential resources should enter the body constantly.
